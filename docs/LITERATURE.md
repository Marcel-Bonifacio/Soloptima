# Literature review

Four journal papers supplied with the project were reviewed in full for their models and forecasting methods. In the text the four papers are called P1 to P4, as numbered in the first table. The two financial models are reviewed separately in `FINANCE.md`.

## Summary of the papers

| | Paper | Problem | Optimiser | Forecasting |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Multi-criteria optimal sizing of PV/wind/fuel cell/battery/diesel for rural electrification in Chad (Abdoulaye et al., 2024 [1]) | Off-grid PV/WT/H₂/battery/DG sizing, hourly rule-based dispatch, 20 years | MOPSO, minimising annual cost and maximising self-sufficiency (SSSI) | None |
| 2 | Optimal power flow in hybrid renewable systems with a hybrid optimiser (Suresh Babu et al., 2025 [2]) | Single-snapshot AC OPF on IEEE-30 with PV/wind (Weibull, lognormal, Monte Carlo) | Hybrid SHO + QAO + GOA ("HSHOA") | None (PV and wind are treated as "forecasted values" with no method given) |
| 3 | Stand-alone PV-wind-battery-diesel sizing with PSO (El Boujdaini et al., 2022 [3]) | Off-grid PV/WT/battery/DG sizing, hourly, 20 years | PSO, minimise COE with LPSP limit | None |
| 4 | Deep-learning solar forecasting for a multi-energy microgrid (Punyam Rajendran and Gebremedhin, 2024 [4]) | Grid-connected heat/electricity microgrid in Norway, scenario sweep in EnergyPLAN (no battery, no diesel, no economics) | Parametric sweep | MLP (3 hidden layers, ReLU, sigmoid output, Adam, MSE) mapping same-hour observed weather to PV power; R² = 0.873 |

Papers 1 to 3 contain no forecasting method. There is no ANN, LSTM, ARIMA or similar model in them, no error metric and no dispatch driven by a forecast. Paper 3 cites the authors' own ANN irradiance forecasting work [5] and also Zhang et al. [6], who size a system using weather forecasts, so those two papers would be the next to read on forecasting.

Soloptima's forecasting therefore follows standard practice rather than these papers (see [MANUAL.md](MANUAL.md), *Forecast-based EMS*). It offers smart persistence, climatology, exponential smoothing, recursive-least-squares regression, an analog ensemble and an adaptive ensemble. The Forecast Lab benchmarks them against each other and shows what each one does to fuel use.

### From paper 4 (Frontiers)

Only the neural network in P4 is relevant to a PV, battery and diesel tool. It was implemented as a causal forecaster, and it differs from the paper in five ways. The network has two hidden layers of 16 ReLU units and a linear output (10-16-16-1) instead of three hidden layers with a sigmoid output. It takes only what is known when the forecast is issued, namely lagged clear-sky indices, a smoothed level, climatology, the lead time and the calendar, whereas the paper feeds in the irradiance observed in the same hour, which makes its model a nowcast rather than a forecast. The calendar is encoded with sine and cosine instead of raw integers. The network is retrained every month on the preceding 330 days in time order, where the paper uses a shuffled split. Its skill is reported against persistence, with night hours left out for PV.

On the example site the network has the best day-ahead PV skill of the real forecasting methods, +0.174 against persistence at 24 h (the perfect and noisy benchmarks are not counted). Its effect on fuel is negligible there, since with any PV method driving the EMS the year-1 fuel stays within 0.01 % of the other methods.

The rest of P4 was not used. Its argument on snow losses only matters in cold climates, its EnergyPLAN priority dispatch is cruder than the existing EMS, and CHP, heat pumps and district heating are outside the scope of the tool.

A few inconsistencies in P4 should be noted. The paper states gradient descent in Eq. 3 but says Adam was used. The chosen hyperparameters are not reported. The validation and test sets are the same data, and Table 4 does not agree with Figs. 9 to 13.

## Corrections applied, where the printed equations are wrong or incomplete

- P2 Eq. 8 (reactive power flow) has the sin and cos terms swapped. The correct form is V_i Σ V_j (G_ij sin θ_ij − B_ij cos θ_ij). Soloptima's load flow is a backward/forward sweep with complex power, so it does not need this equation directly.
- In P2 Eq. 19 the term "θ_i − θ_i" is read as θ_i − θ_j.
- P2 samples Weibull wind speeds as v = C (ln r)^(1/k), which is invalid. Soloptima uses v = C (−ln(1 − r))^(1/k).
- The first branch of P2 Eq. 23 (PV) is printed with H², while the standard form is P_sr · H / H_std. Soloptima uses its own transposition, thermal and loss-chain model instead.
- The numerator of P2 Eq. 39 (QAO) uses squared differences. Soloptima uses the correct three-point quadratic minimiser, x* = ½ [(q2² − q3²) f1 + (q3² − q1²) f2 + (q1² − q2²) f3] / [(q2 − q3) f1 + (q3 − q1) f2 + (q1 − q2) f3], guarded against a near-zero denominator and clipped to the variable bounds.
- The SHO update in P2 leaves out the cluster average. Soloptima implements the canonical form [7], in which the new position is the mean over the best-N cluster of P_k − E·|B·P_k − X| and h falls linearly from 5 to 0.
- P2 Eq. 46 for GOA is the raw swarm model, which cannot be used for optimisation as it stands. Soloptima uses the optimisation form [8], x_i = c·Σ c·(ub − lb)/2 · s(d_ij)·(x_j − x_i)/d_ij + T̂, with c falling from 1 to 10⁻⁵ and s(r) = 0.5 e^(−r/1.5) − e^(−r).
- In P1 Eq. 11 the discharge should be divided by η_d, and P1 Fig. 4 prints "E_bmax + E_b" where it should be a minus sign. Soloptima's battery applies √RTE in each direction.
- P1 Eq. 1 applies both P_r and η_PV, which counts the efficiency twice, and P1 Eq. 41 (LOLP) divides energy by hours. Neither form was used.
- The CRF formula of P3 Eq. 12 mixes rates. Soloptima uses CRF = i(1 + i)^N / ((1 + i)^N − 1) with one discount rate throughout.
- In P3 Eq. 19 the deficit of each hour is clamped at zero or above.
- None of papers 1 to 3 says how constraints are handled. Soloptima adds a static penalty, so an infeasible point scores the diesel-only cost × (1 + 10 × normalised violation). It then ranks behind every feasible system cheaper than diesel only, and the reported optimum is always feasible. The constraints covered are unmet load, the minimum renewable share, curtailment, the land limit and capex.
- Where the papers leave settings out, Soloptima uses documented defaults. PSO runs with w falling from 0.9 to 0.4, c1 = c2 = 2 and a 20 % velocity clamp. MOPSO keeps a 60-solution archive pruned by crowding distance, picks leaders by binary tournament and uses a decaying mutation. Population and iterations are left to the user.

## Not taken over

P2's single-snapshot metaheuristic OPF was not taken over. It has no storage and no coupling in time, and running a metaheuristic every hour is not needed on a small radial collector network, so the dispatch keeps its own unit commitment and EMS. P1's hydrogen chain (electrolyser, tank and fuel cell) is outside the PV, battery and diesel scope, although it could be added later as another kind of storage.

None of the numerical results of papers 1 to 3 is used as a default, because all three contain internal inconsistencies. The renewable cases of P2 miss the power balance by about 50 MW. The costs in P1 do not reconcile and its energy balance does not close. In P3 the wind energy values are mistyped by a factor of 10 and some renewable fractions exceed 1. The job-creation factors of P1 are used, but these are inputs and not results.

## Second reading list, review and gaps (November 2026)

Twenty-one papers and one standard were read in full, and each method was checked against the Soloptima code before it was called a gap. Two further conference papers on the list evaluate a commercial PV design tool by name. They are left out of this document, in line with the project's rule of not naming other software, and one of them (a one-year comparison of measured and simulated output from a 12 kWp array) is mentioned below without a citation.

The list is uneven. The two standards-grade sources, IEEE Std 2800-2022 [9] and the solar-pump study in *Applied Energy* [10], are carefully done. Most of the sizing papers contain numbers that do not reconcile with each other, as the notes per paper show, so none of their results is used as a default, as with the first list. Their methods are a different matter, and several of them point at things Soloptima did not yet do.

### Gaps, ranked by value to a planner and by effort

The ranking weighs how much a gap would change a sizing decision against the work and the data it needs. Effort is S (days), M (one to two weeks) or L (longer). Each gap ends with a note on what was built in November 2026.

1. **The optimiser does not size the gensets or the PV inverter, and so cannot compare architectures** [11], [12], [13], [14]. All four sizing papers rank whole system types, for instance by dropping the diesel or adding wind. Soloptima's search varied PV, battery energy, C-rate and wind units, while the genset fleet was fixed and the inverter followed the DC/AC ratio. Adding the number of genset units (and perhaps the unit size) and the DC/AC ratio to the search would answer a question users ask early, which is how many gensets the plant really needs once PV and storage are in. Effort M, in `engine/src/optimizer/` and `engine/src/sim/fleet.rs`. *The optimiser now sizes the genset count, with zero allowed, and reports the best design for each system type. The DC/AC ratio is still not searched, because the PV capex already includes the inverters and the cost data cannot price a smaller one (see the known limitations in the manual).*

2. **Inverters are sized on kW, not kVA, and the load flow ignores reactive power** [9], [19]. Inverter and PCS ratings in the electrical design were active-power ratings, and the 8760 h load flow injected no reactive power from PV, battery or gensets. Where a grid code asks for a 0.95 power factor at full output (IEEE 2800 sets at least 0.3287 times the rated active power in both directions), the inverter count would probably come out 5 to 15 % short, and more behind high-reactance transformers. A kVA field, the capability check S ≥ √(P² + Q²) at the point of connection and reactive injection in the load flow would close this. Effort M, in `engine/src/electrical/` (`design.rs`, `loadflow.rs`) and `ui/src/lib/equip/library.js`. *Inverter and PCS datasheets now take a kVA rating, the design checks the capability against a power factor at the point of connection, and the load flow carries the reactive power. Transformers and cables are still sized on kW.*

3. **The battery cannot charge from the grid** [17]. The manual stated this as a limitation. On weak grids with scheduled load shedding, which Soloptima already models, filling the battery before a known outage would replace diesel, and with time-of-use tariffs it would allow arbitrage. The predictive EMS already prices grid energy hour by hour, so the change sits mostly in its cost function and in the rule-based dispatch. Effort M, in `engine/src/sim/planner.rs`, `dispatch.rs` and `grid.rs`. *Grid charging is now an option, off by default. The battery charges before a scheduled outage and when a time-of-use spread pays, both in the rule-based dispatch and in the predictive EMS.*

4. **No comparison of measured plant output with the model.** The one paper on the list with field data (one year from a 12 kWp array, not cited here for the reason given above) found monthly deviations within about 10 % in warm months and over 35 % in November. Soloptima's validation rests on parity with its own earlier engine and a cross-check against PVGIS, and neither of these is field data. Importing metered AC output, reporting the monthly deviation, MBE and RMSE, and fitting soiling and availability to it would give users a way to calibrate before an expansion. Effort M, in `engine/src/series/`, a new `pv/reconcile.rs` and `docs/VALIDATION.md`. *Metered PV output can now be imported and compared with the model by hour and by month (MBE, RMSE, R² and monthly deviation). Outage hours are flagged, and the fitted soiling and availability can be applied to the project. Partial outages and degradation between years are not handled yet.*

5. **No flexible or deferrable load** [13], [10], [25]. Irrigation and water pumping with a tank can absorb surplus PV and so shrink the battery. The pump physics in [10], a hydraulic balance with Theis drawdown and a power law Q = aP^b fitted per site, is the most careful treatment on the list. A first version would need a daily energy or water demand, a power limit, a time window and a tank, and the predictive EMS would gain a state. Effort M, in `engine/src/model.rs`, `sim/dispatch.rs` and `ems/`. *A deferrable load is now an option, off by default, with a daily energy, a power limit, a time window and an optional store that can be entered as water. It takes PV and wind surplus first, and the predictive EMS places it in the cheapest hours. The pump law Q = aP^b is not used (see the limitations in the manual).*

6. **Battery ageing ignores depth of discharge** [21]. Cycle fade was linear in throughput at one rated depth of discharge, so shallow cycling earned no extra life. Rainflow cycle counting with a curve of cycles against depth of discharge, taken from two to four datasheet points, would change the replacement years and the cycle cost the EMS uses. Effort M, in `engine/src/sim/dispatch.rs` and `sim/planner.rs`. *The battery can now age by such a curve, off by default, with rainflow counting after ASTM E1049-85, Miner's rule and a matching cycle cost in the EMS.*

7. **No grid-extension break-even** [11], [21]. Neither the economics nor the grid inputs carried a connection cost or a cost per kilometre, so the tool could not say at what distance an off-grid hybrid becomes cheaper than extending the line, which is often the first question in rural electrification. Effort S, in `engine/src/model.rs` and `sim/econ.rs`. *For an off-grid project the tool now gives the line length at which grid supply costs the same NPC as the plant. It is off until a cost per kilometre is entered.*

8. **No weak-grid screening** [24], [9]. The utility short-circuit power was already an input but only fed the fault calculation. A short-circuit ratio at the point of connection, with a warning below a user threshold (IEEE 2800 sets none), and an estimate of the voltage dip when a transformer is energised would flag sites that need a stability study. Effort S, in `engine/src/electrical/fault.rs`. *Both the short-circuit ratio and the energising dip are now reported, each with its own warning threshold.*

9. **Commitment without start-up cost or minimum up and down time** [18]. The planner's state was the stored energy, and start costs appeared only in the economics. Carrying a start penalty, or the units online, in the planner would reduce genset cycling and give more realistic start counts. Effort M, in `engine/src/sim/planner.rs` and `fleet.rs`. *Minimum up and down times now hold in the dispatch, and the predictive plan can carry the cost per start. Both are off by default.*

10. **Reliability is reported in energy, not in time** [12], [22]. Soloptima reported LPSP, loss-of-load hours and events, and autonomy at mean load. It lacked the worst run of consecutive low-PV days in the weather record and an option to require N days of autonomy, both of which are common in tenders. Effort S, in `engine/src/sim/summary.rs` and `optimizer/eval.rs`. *The results now give the longest run of low-PV days and of unserved hours, and the optimiser can require N days of autonomy.*

11. **Sensitivity is one-dimensional and cannot scale the resource** [12], [13]. Neither irradiance nor wind speed could be scaled, and there was no two-way map of the winning architecture over, for example, fuel price and load. Effort S for resource scaling and M for the maps, in `engine/src/optimizer/`. *Irradiance and wind speed can now be scaled in the sensitivity cases and the tornado, and a two-way map either re-optimises or re-simulates each cell.*

12. **MPP tracking is assumed perfect** [26], [28], [27]. The partial-shade model picks the global maximum. Under shade, P&O and incremental conductance can settle on a local maximum [26], and lab tests under fluctuating irradiance report dynamic tracking efficiencies of 94 to 99 % [28]. These figures are upper bounds for lab profiles rather than annual losses, so a tracking loss set by the user, with a small default, is probably the honest first step, followed by an optional local-maximum rule in the bypass-diode model. Effort S, then M, in `engine/src/pv/profile.rs` and `engine/src/shading.rs`. *A tracking loss can now be set (zero by default), and the tracker can be made to stop at a local maximum under building and tree shade.*

13. **No frequency reserve in grid-connected operation** [9]. Off-grid, battery power already counts as spinning reserve. Grid-connected, nothing held battery or PV headroom for primary or fast frequency response. A reserve in kW and minutes, held as a floor on the state of charge and as power headroom, would show its cost in energy and its revenue. Effort M, in `engine/src/sim/dispatch.rs` and `sim/planner.rs`. *Such a reserve is now an option, off by default. It is held as battery power headroom and an energy band, and its revenue enters the lifecycle economics.*

14. **Forecasting could use more than one weather model and sub-hourly steps** [16], [15]. The live schedule took one numerical weather model without any correction against site data, and the forecasts are hourly while the dispatch can run in 15-minute steps. Blending several models with the existing recursive-least-squares code, and reporting forecast error by weather class (clear, cloudy, overcast), are the parts that suit a Rust tool. The deep-learning architectures themselves do not, and their reported gains are measured against weak baselines. Effort S to M, in `engine/src/weather/forecast.rs` and `ems/bench.rs`. *This gap is only partly closed. The live schedule can blend both weather providers, but with fixed weights, since the live path has no site history to learn them from. The forecast benchmark now reports its errors by weather class.*

15. **No PV ramp-rate smoothing** [20]. Only genset ramps were limited. Sizing a battery for a ramp limit in % per minute at the point of connection needs minute data, which Soloptima does not take, and the hourly treatment in [20] does not show ramps either. Effort L, or M for a coarse 15-minute version. *The coarse version is now built and off by default. At 15 and 30 minute steps a limit in % of the PV and wind rating per minute is held by the battery, and what it cannot hold is curtailed or counted as a violation, with an optional limit in the optimiser. Hourly weather spread over these steps holds no cloud ramps, so the results should be read as a lower bound.*

### Not taken forward

The CNN-LSTM, temporal convolutional and multi-agent reinforcement-learning methods [15], [16], [19] were not taken forward. They need heavy machine-learning dependencies, and on the evidence given they would not change a sizing decision. The equilibrium optimizer [12] was also left out, since the paper gives no evidence that it does better than PSO at equal reliability. Converter control, MPPT algorithms and oscillation damping [13], [24], [25], [27], [28] are questions of equipment design and sit below the resolution of an energy model. Heat and hydrogen [11] are outside the PV, battery and diesel scope, as with the first list. The DC-microgrid survey [23] is too rough to support a DC-coupled option, and if such an option is built it should rest on better sources.

## Third reading list, review and gaps (November 2026)

Ten journal papers were read in full and checked against the Soloptima code. Six of them take their results from commercial design tools, and the title of [29] names two of these tools, so it is shortened in the reference list in line with the project's rule of not naming other software. None of the ten compares its results with measured plant data.

As with the earlier lists, the quality varies a good deal. In [29] the "100 % renewable" optimum leaves about half of the load unserved (9.44 of 18.4 GWh a year), and the abstract quotes sizes that match none of the tables. The emissions in [30] appear to be about ten times too low for the fuel it reports. The PV yield in [35] implies a performance ratio above 1, and every minimum voltage reported in [31] lies below that paper's own limit of 0.95 pu. These results are therefore not used. The most careful sources are the resilience study [34], the islanded-microgrid planning study [32] and the optimisation tool of [37], which is also the closest to Soloptima in purpose.

### Gaps, ranked by value to a planner and by effort

The numbering continues from the second list, and effort is again S (days), M (one to two weeks) or L (longer).

16. **Genset wear-out is not costed** [29], [30], [36], [37], [38]. Run hours were counted, but no genset was overhauled or replaced when it reached its life, which most of the papers set at 15 000 h. In [37] the engine is replaced at 3 000 to 7 500 h and the alternator at 20 000 h. A hybrid plant cuts run hours sharply, so leaving wear out probably understates its benefit. An overhaul interval and cost per unit, and a replacement life in hours, would close the gap. Effort S, in `engine/src/sim/econ.rs` and `model.rs`. *Overhauls and replacements by run hours are now booked for each genset group, off by default. The hours left at the end count as salvage, and the diesel-only baseline wears out in the same way.*

17. **No outage survival measure** [34], [35]. Soloptima drew random grid outages but reported only their effect in energy. For every start hour of the year and outage lengths of 1 to 336 h, [34] reports the probability that the critical load is still served, and sums it up as the minimum, the 5th percentile, the mean and the share of the year below 90 %. Its genset model, R(t) = OA·(1 − FTS)·e^(−t/MTTF), rests on failure-to-start and mean-time-to-failure data from 543 units. A sweep of this kind, with a probability of failing to start added to the genset outage model, would give tenders the figure they usually ask for. Effort M, in `engine/src/sim/` and a new resilience module. *A separate outage survival study now covers every start hour and outages of up to 720 h. It allows for gensets failing to start and failing while running, and it is computed exactly over the combinations of working units.*

18. **No critical-load tier** [34], [35], [32]. Every kilowatt of load counted the same. A critical share that is served first during outages and shortfalls, with the rest shed, would change the battery and genset sizes needed for resilience, and [32] goes further by weighting the shed load by priority in its objective. Effort M, in `model.rs`, `sim/dispatch.rs` and `sim/planner.rs`. *A critical share of the load can now be set, off by default, and it is shed last. The plant can also be told to serve only the critical load during grid outages, and the optimiser's reliability limits then apply to the critical load.*

19. **Pollutants are incomplete and unpriced** [37], [29], [35], [36]. NOx and SOx were fixed per litre, PM2.5 and CO were missing, and only CO2 carried a price. In [37] NOx and PM2.5 depend on engine size and loading and are priced per gram, and [36] adds an embodied factor for PV energy. Prices per gram and a PM2.5 factor are small changes, while the dependence on loading takes a little more. Effort S, in `sim/dispatch.rs` and `sim/econ.rs`. *PM2.5 and CO factors, a price per kg for each pollutant and an embodied CO2 factor for PV energy are now inputs, all off by default. The part-load correction was left out (see the limitations in the manual).*

20. **Genset unit size is not searched** [37]. The optimiser sized the genset count, but the rating per unit stayed fixed. [37] picks units from a catalogue of seven sizes, and Soloptima's genset datasheet library could serve as that catalogue. Effort M, in `engine/src/optimizer/`. *The optimiser can now search the unit rating of the first genset group, off by default, from a typed list or from the genset datasheets, each with its own minimum load, fuel curve and price.*

21. **Optimiser results come from one run** [31], [36]. The metaheuristics reported a single run, whereas both papers repeat each method 10 to 50 times and give the best, mean, worst and standard deviation. A measure of Pareto quality and a table of the front at fixed LPSP targets (0, 1, 2, 5 and 10 %) would also help, as would LPSP and emissions as MOPSO objectives. Effort S, in `engine/src/optimizer/`. *Repeat runs are now available with their best, mean, worst and standard deviation, all off by default. MOPSO can take unmet load or CO2 as its second objective, reports spacing and hypervolume and gives the front at fixed LPSP targets, and a fuzzy best compromise can be chosen instead of the knee.*

22. **No check that results make physical sense** [35], [30], [29]. Three papers publish results that fail simple checks of specific yield against irradiation, emissions per litre of fuel, LCOE units and the consistency of the payback. A check of the results that lists what lies outside a plausible range would catch the same slips in user projects. Effort S, in `engine/src/sim/summary.rs` and the results page. *Every simulation now checks the performance ratio, capacity factors, fuel per kWh, emissions per litre, energy balance, LCOE, payback, battery cycles and round trip against stated ranges, and lists anything that falls outside them.*

23. **No islanded N-1 feeder screen** [32]. For each radial branch outage, a screen would check that every island left behind has a battery inverter or genset able to form the grid, and enough capacity for its load. An islanded droop load flow, with frequency as an unknown and loads that depend on voltage and frequency, would come later. Effort S for the screen and L for the islanded load flow, in `engine/src/electrical/`. *The screen is now built and off by default. For each feeder outage, the island that keeps the load is checked for a grid-forming source and firm capacity, and the load and critical load that would be shed are reported. The islanded droop load flow is not built.*

24. **No unbalanced-fault screen** [33]. The paper gives closed forms for the positive- and negative-sequence voltages and the voltage unbalance factor at the point of connection, from the grid voltages, the impedance and its R/X and the inverter current limit, and it limits the sum of both sequence currents. The second part bears on the reactive capability check of gap 2. Effort S, in `engine/src/electrical/`. *The screen is now built and only reports. When it was checked against our own derivation for a Thevenin source, the closed forms for V+ and V− held, but the mode test did not. It has to leave room for the positive-sequence current under the shared limit, so it reads Vg− ≤ Z·(Imax − |I+|) rather than Vg− ≤ Z·Imax, and the two only agree when V+ is 0.9 pu or more.*

25. **Load and reporting extras** [30], [38], [29]. These are a seasonal load builder from an appliance list (items, power and hours a day), a histogram of battery SOC by band and season, a monthly genset table (hours, starts, capacity factor and efficiency), and the renewable fraction by capacity, energy and peak. Effort S each, in the interface. *All four are now built, with the appliance list on the Load page.*

### Not taken forward

The one-day MILP with K-means representative days [37] was not taken forward, because a full-year hourly simulation already covers seasons and storage over several days, which a single representative day cannot. The cost-per-step dispatch rules of [29] and [30] choose greedily each hour, and the predictive EMS already plans ahead. Siting distributed generators on a utility feeder [31] is a question of network planning rather than plant design. Fuel blends [37] could come later as part of gap 19, but nobody has asked for them so far. Tuning the droop gains of grid-forming inverters [32], [33] is controller design and sits below the resolution of an energy model.
