# Literature review

This review covers the published work that Soloptima's models are built on. It keeps only the sources whose equations, methods or findings are used in the tool, and for each one it explains what was taken, why, and where the source falls short. Every source was read in full and each method was checked against the Soloptima code before it was counted as used. Papers that were read but contributed nothing to the tool are left out. The two industry financial models are reviewed separately in [FINANCE.md](FINANCE.md).

The sources fall into two groups of very different quality. The interconnection standard IEEE 2800 [1], the solar-pump study [2], the resilience study [3], the islanded-microgrid planning study [4] and the diesel-PV-battery optimisation tool [5] are carefully done, and their values can be relied on. Most of the sizing case studies are not. Their published numbers often fail to reconcile with each other, as the notes below show, so none of their results is used as a default in Soloptima. Their methods are a different matter, and several of them pointed at things a planner needs that Soloptima did not yet do. This split runs through the whole review. A method is judged on whether it is sound and whether it would change a sizing decision, and a published result is only trusted after it passes simple physical checks.

The developments that came out of the review are numbered as gaps 1 to 25, the same numbers the manual, the code and the tests use. They are listed together at the end of this document.

## Component models

**PV.** The cell temperature follows the NOCT model, T_c = T_a + (NOCT − 20)/800 · G, as in Abdoulaye et al. [6] (Eq. 2) and El Boujdaini et al. [7] (Eq. 2). The PV output model of [6] was not used, because its Eq. 1 applies both the rated power and the module efficiency and so counts the efficiency twice. Soloptima keeps its own transposition, thermal and loss-chain model instead.

The partial-shade model picks the global maximum of the power curve, which assumes the inverter always finds it. The review of tracking methods by Kumar et al. [8] shows that perturb and observe and incremental conductance can settle on a local maximum under partial shade, and the laboratory tests of Priyadarshi et al. [9] report dynamic tracking efficiencies of 94 to 99 % under changing irradiance. Neither figure can be used as an annual loss. The ratings in [8] are qualitative and partly inconsistent, and the tests in [9] run on an emulator with synthetic irradiance steps, so they are upper bounds for laboratory profiles. Soloptima therefore takes the tracking loss as an input, zero by default, and offers an optional rule that stops the tracker at a local maximum under building and tree shade (gap 12).

**Wind.** The turbine power curve is cubic between cut-in and rated speed and flat up to cut-out, with a linear variant, following [6] (Eqs. 3 and 4), [7] (Eq. 3) and Suresh Babu et al. [10] (Eq. 27). Wind speed is carried to hub height with the power law v = v_ref (H/H_ref)^α and α = 1/7 ([6], Eq. 5).

**Battery.** Self-discharge follows [6] (Eqs. 10 and 11), SOC(t) = SOC(t − 1)(1 − σ) ± the charge or discharge term. As printed, Eq. 11 does not divide the discharge by the discharge efficiency, and Fig. 4 has a plus where a minus belongs. Soloptima applies the square root of the round-trip efficiency in each direction.

Cycle fade used to be linear in throughput at one rated depth of discharge, so a battery that cycled shallowly earned no extra life. Zabelinskiy et al. [11] size their storage with a cycle life that depends on the depth of discharge, which is how datasheets state it. Their economics are weak, with costs inflated over time but not discounted and a constant diesel consumption, so only the ageing approach was taken. The battery can now age by a curve of cycles against depth of discharge built from datasheet points, with rainflow counting after ASTM E1049-85, Miner's rule, and a matching cycle cost in the predictive EMS (gap 6).

**Diesel generators.** Fuel use follows the linear curve F = A·P_out + B·P_rated with A = 0.246 and B = 0.08145 L/kWh ([6], Eq. 25, and [7], Eq. 11), next to Soloptima's part-load tables.

Run hours were counted, but no genset was ever overhauled or replaced. Most of the case studies set a genset life of about 15 000 h [12], [13], [14], [15], and Rangel et al. [5] replace the engine every 3 000 to 7 500 h and the alternator at 20 000 h. Since a hybrid plant cuts run hours sharply, leaving wear out of the costs understates its benefit. Soloptima now books overhauls and replacements by run hours for each genset group, counts the hours left at the end as salvage, and wears the diesel-only baseline out in the same way so that the comparison stays fair (gap 16).

## Dispatch and operation

Soloptima keeps its own hourly unit commitment and predictive EMS rather than the dispatch of any one paper. The single-snapshot optimal power flow of [10] has no storage and no coupling in time, and a metaheuristic run every hour adds nothing on a small radial collector network. The cost-per-step rules of [12] and [13] choose greedily each hour, whereas the predictive EMS plans ahead. The MILP of [5] works on one representative day chosen by K-means, which cannot show seasons or storage over several days, both of which a full-year hourly simulation covers. Within that frame the papers below changed how the dispatch behaves.

**Genset commitment.** Ahmed and Jiang [16] carry start-up costs and minimum up and down times in a day-ahead commitment. Their cost coefficients are not given and their storage result contradicts their conclusion, so only the formulation was taken. Minimum up and down times now hold in the dispatch, and the predictive plan can carry a cost per start (gap 9). Both reduce genset cycling and give more realistic start counts.

**Charging from the grid.** Šimić et al. [17] schedule a battery to charge from the grid in a microgrid with a genset. The study covers two days only and its "linear" programme contains products of binary variables, but the principle is sound and Soloptima lacked it. On weak grids with scheduled load shedding, filling the battery before a known outage replaces diesel, and under time-of-use tariffs it allows arbitrage. Both are now options in the rule-based dispatch and the predictive EMS (gap 3).

**Frequency reserve.** IEEE 2800 [1] expects inverter-based plants to provide primary and fast frequency response. Off-grid, battery power already counted as spinning reserve, but nothing was held back in grid-connected operation. The reserve is now held as battery power headroom and an energy band, and its revenue enters the lifecycle economics (gap 13).

**Ramp limits.** Morampudi et al. [18] size a battery to smooth the output of a 100 MWp PV plant, but they do it on hourly data, which cannot show ramps at all. The idea is right and the method is not. Soloptima limits the ramp of PV and wind in % of rating per minute at 15 and 30 minute steps, holds it with the battery and curtails or flags what the battery cannot hold (gap 15). Hourly weather spread over shorter steps holds no cloud ramps either, so these results are a lower bound, and the manual says so.

**Deferrable load.** Water pumping and irrigation with a tank can absorb surplus PV and so reduce the battery a site needs. Rehman et al. [19] treat such loads as demand-side management, and Zuffinetti et al. [2] give the most careful pump physics in the review, a hydraulic balance with Theis drawdown and a power law Q = aP^b fitted to each site. A fitted law of that kind needs borehole data that users rarely have at the planning stage, so Soloptima models the load by what users do know, a daily energy or water demand, a power limit, a time window and an optional store. It takes PV and wind surplus first, and the predictive EMS places it in the cheapest hours (gap 5).

**Critical load.** Marqusee et al. [3] and Aeggegn et al. [20] separate a critical load that must be served through an outage from the rest, and Shabar et al. [4] weight shed load by priority. Every kilowatt used to count the same in Soloptima. A critical share can now be set, which is shed last, and the plant can be told to serve only the critical load during grid outages, in which case the reliability limits of the optimiser apply to the critical load (gap 18).

## Sizing and optimisation

**Algorithms.** The particle swarm optimiser follows [7] (Eqs. 21 and 22), with a global best and an inertia weight. The multi-objective version follows [6] (Eq. 50), which traces a Pareto front of cost against self-sufficiency. The hybrid of spotted-hyena search, quadratic approximation and grasshopper optimisation follows [10], but three of its printed update rules could not be used as they stand, so the canonical forms of Dhiman and Kumar [21] and Saremi et al. [22] were used instead. The corrections are listed further down. None of [6] to [7] states how constraints are handled or which settings were used. Soloptima scores an infeasible design at the diesel-only cost times one plus ten times its normalised violation, so it ranks behind every feasible design that beats diesel and the reported optimum is always feasible. Where settings are missing, documented defaults are used, with the inertia weight falling from 0.9 to 0.4, c1 = c2 = 2, a 20 % velocity clamp and a 60-solution archive pruned by crowding distance.

**What the search varies.** The four sizing studies of Alluraiah and Vijayapriya [23], Kharrich et al. [24], Rehman et al. [19] and Güven et al. [25] all rank whole system types, for instance with and without diesel or wind. Soloptima's search varied PV, battery energy, C-rate and wind, while the genset fleet stayed fixed, so it could not answer a question users ask early on, which is how many gensets the plant still needs once PV and storage are in. The optimiser now sizes the genset count, with zero allowed, and reports the best design for each system type (gap 1). Rangel et al. [5] go a step further and choose each unit from a catalogue of sizes. Soloptima's genset datasheet library serves as that catalogue, and the unit rating of the first genset group can now be searched, each candidate with its own minimum load, fuel curve and price (gap 20). The DC/AC ratio is still not searched, because the PV cost already includes the inverters and the cost data cannot price a smaller one.

**How results are reported.** The sizing studies are also a lesson in how not to compare algorithms. In [24] the algorithms are compared at different reliability, an LPSP of 4.89 % against 0.017 %, from one run each, which says nothing about which algorithm is better. The "multi-objective" optimisation of [25] is a weighted sum of three cost measures. A metaheuristic gives a different answer on every run, so Ojo et al. [26] and Gharib and Mohammadbeigi [14] repeat each method 10 to 50 times and report the best, mean, worst and standard deviation. Soloptima now does the same on request. MOPSO can also take unmet load or CO2 as its second objective, reports the spacing and hypervolume of the front and gives the front at fixed LPSP targets, and a fuzzy best compromise after [26] can be chosen instead of the knee (gap 21). The spacing metric is read the wrong way round in [14], where a lower value means a more even front. For the same reason the equilibrium optimiser of [24] was not added, since the paper gives no evidence that it beats PSO at equal reliability.

**Sensitivity.** Sensitivity in Soloptima was one-dimensional and could not scale the resource, while [24] and [19] show how strongly the winning design moves with irradiance, wind speed and fuel price. Irradiance and wind speed can now be scaled in the sensitivity cases and the tornado, and a two-way map either re-optimises or re-simulates each cell (gap 11).

## Reliability and resilience

The energy-based indicators follow the papers. The loss of power supply probability is LPSP = Σ deficit / Σ load ([7], Eq. 19), with each hourly deficit clamped at zero, which the printed equation leaves out. Self-consumption and self-sufficiency are SSCI = Σ min(load, RE) / Σ RE and SSSI = Σ min(load, RE) / Σ load ([6], Eqs. 39 and 40), and the renewable penetration is REP = 100 (1 − LPSP) ([6], Eq. 47). The LOLP of [6] (Eq. 41) was not used, because it divides energy by hours.

Energy measures hide how long a shortfall lasts. Kharrich et al. [24] use days of autonomy, which tenders often ask for, and Soloptima only reported autonomy at mean load. The results now give the longest run of low-PV days in the weather record and the longest run of unserved hours, and the optimiser can require N days of autonomy (gap 10).

The most useful source on resilience is Marqusee et al. [3]. For every start hour of the year and outages of 1 to 336 h, they report the probability that the critical load is still served, summed up as the minimum, the 5th percentile, the mean and the share of the year below 90 %. Their genset model, R(t) = OA·(1 − FTS)·e^(−t/MTTF), rests on failure-to-start and mean-time-to-failure data from 543 units, which is far better evidence than the assumed availabilities of the other papers. Two parts of their method are optimistic, the perfect-foresight dispatch and a genset pool without a minimum load. Soloptima therefore keeps its own dispatch and adopts the measure and the failure data. The outage survival study covers every start hour and outages of up to 720 h, allows for gensets that fail to start or fail while running, and is computed exactly over the combinations of working units (gap 17). Aeggegn et al. [20] take the yearly interruption hours as an input, which is how most users know their grid.

## Economics and emissions

The annualised indicators follow [6] and [7]. The capital recovery factor is CRF = i(1 + i)^N / ((1 + i)^N − 1), the total annualised cost is TAC = NPC · CRF and the cost of energy is COE = TAC divided by the year-1 energy served ([6], Eqs. 29, 38, 43 and 44, and [7], Eqs. 12 to 15). Eq. 12 of [7] mixes two rates, and Soloptima uses one discount rate throughout. These indicators sit next to the discounted-energy LCOE rather than replacing it. The development indicators also come from [6], namely jobs per MW of PV (2.70) and wind (1.10) and per MWh of battery (0.01) (Eq. 48, Table 4), and a human development index from the electricity per person, 0.0978 ln(E/person) − 0.0319 (Eq. 49). The job factors are inputs to the paper rather than its results, which is why they could be used when its results could not.

**Grid extension.** In rural electrification the first question is often whether to build a plant at all or extend the line. Alluraiah and Vijayapriya [23] and Zabelinskiy et al. [11] cost the grid alternative per kilometre. For an off-grid project Soloptima now gives the line length at which grid supply costs the same NPC as the plant, once a cost per kilometre is entered (gap 7).

**Emissions.** CO2, NOx and SOx are counted per litre of diesel after [6] (Eqs. 28 and 46), but only CO2 carried a price. Rangel et al. [5] price NOx and PM2.5 per gram, and [14] adds the embodied emissions of PV energy. PM2.5 and CO factors, a price per kilogram for each pollutant and an embodied CO2 factor for PV are now inputs (gap 19). In [5] the NOx and PM2.5 rates also depend on engine size and loading. That correction was left out, because it needs engine data most users do not have, and the manual lists it as a limitation.

## Electrical design and grid integration

**Load flow.** The network equations, the real-power loss and the voltage deviation VD = Σ|V − V_ref| follow [10] (Eqs. 7, 18 and 19). Soloptima solves the radial collector network with a backward and forward sweep in complex power over all 8 760 hours This form does not need the reactive power equation of the paper, which is printed with the sine and cosine terms swapped.

**Reactive power.** Inverter and PCS ratings used to be active-power ratings, and the load flow carried no reactive power from the plant. Where a grid code asks for reactive capability at full output, the inverter count comes out short. IEEE 2800 [1] sets at least 0.3287 times the rated active power in both directions, and Hu et al. [27] show how much inverter reactive power does for the voltage on a feeder. Their learned controller does worse than their own model-based optimum on both cost and voltage violations, which supports using a model-based check in a planning tool. Inverters and PCS now take a kVA rating, the design checks S ≥ √(P² + Q²) against a power factor at the point of connection, and the load flow carries the reactive power (gap 2). Transformers and cables are still sized on kW.

**Weak grids.** Dong et al. [28] show that PV on a weak AC grid can excite sub-synchronous oscillation. Their study is a simulation that never states the grid strength in numbers, and IEEE 2800 [1] sets no threshold either, so neither gives a value to check against. What a planning tool can do is flag the sites that need a stability study. Soloptima now reports the short-circuit ratio at the point of connection and the voltage dip when a transformer is energised, each against a threshold the user sets (gap 8).

**Islanded operation.** Shabar et al. [4] place and size grid-forming generators so that every island left after a single feeder outage can still carry its load. Their full method is a steady-state optimisation that needs a computing cluster, but its first step is a screen that a planning tool can run. For each feeder outage Soloptima now checks that the island that keeps the load has a grid-forming source and enough firm capacity, and reports the load and critical load that would be shed (gap 23). Their islanded droop load flow, with frequency as an unknown and loads that depend on voltage and frequency, is not built.

**Unbalanced faults.** Wang et al. [29] give closed forms for the positive- and negative-sequence voltages and the voltage unbalance factor at the point of connection when the inverters support both sequences within one current limit. Checked against our own derivation for a Thevenin source, the closed forms for V+ and V− hold, but the test for the operating mode does not. It has to leave room for the positive-sequence current under the shared limit, so it reads Vg− ≤ Z·(Imax − |I+|) rather than Vg− ≤ Z·Imax, and the two only agree when V+ is 0.9 pu or more. Soloptima uses the corrected test in a screen that only reports (gap 24).

## Forecasting

None of [6] to [7] contains a forecasting method. There is no neural network, ARIMA or similar model in them, no error measure and no dispatch driven by a forecast. Soloptima's forecasting therefore follows standard practice, with smart persistence, climatology, exponential smoothing, recursive least squares, an analog ensemble and an adaptive ensemble, and the Forecast Lab benchmarks them against each other and against the fuel they save (see [MANUAL.md](MANUAL.md), *Forecast-based EMS*).

The one forecasting model taken from a paper is the neural network of Punyam Rajendran and Gebremedhin [30]. As published it is not a forecast. It maps the weather observed in the same hour to PV power, so it is a nowcast, and its validation and test sets are the same data. The paper also states gradient descent in one place and Adam in another, does not report its chosen hyperparameters, and its Table 4 does not agree with its Figs. 9 to 13. Soloptima keeps the idea of a small network and changes five things. It uses two hidden layers of 16 ReLU units and a linear output, takes only what is known when the forecast is issued (lagged clear-sky indices, a smoothed level, climatology, the lead time and the calendar), encodes the calendar with sine and cosine, retrains every month on the preceding 330 days in time order rather than on a shuffled split, and reports its skill against persistence with the night hours left out. On the example site it has the best day-ahead PV skill of the real methods, +0.174 against persistence at 24 h, yet the year-1 fuel stays within 0.01 % of the other methods. Forecast skill and fuel saved are not the same thing, which is the reason the Forecast Lab reports both. The rest of [30], a heat and electricity scenario sweep without a battery, diesel or economics, is outside the scope of the tool.

Li et al. [31] blend five numerical weather models with cloud tracking for 95 PV farms and report their error by weather class. The deep-learning framework itself was not taken, because it needs heavy dependencies, its data are not public and its reconciliation makes the 5 and 30 minute forecasts worse on cloudy days. Two of its ideas suit Soloptima. The live schedule can now blend both weather providers, with fixed weights since the live path has no site history to learn them from, and the forecast benchmark reports its errors for clear, cloudy and overcast days (gap 14).

## Validation and reporting

The weakest point of the literature is validation. None of the case studies in the review compares its results with measured plant output, and several publish results that fail simple physical checks. The "100 % renewable" optimum of AL Dawsari et al. [12] leaves about half the load unserved (9.44 of 18.4 GWh a year), and its abstract quotes sizes that match none of its tables. The emissions in Al Garni et al. [13] are about ten times too low for the fuel they report. The PV yield of Aeggegn et al. [20] implies a performance ratio above 1, and its load and LCOE differ by up to ten times between sections. The internal results of [6] to [7] do not hold together either. The renewable cases of [10] miss the power balance by about 50 MW, the costs in [6] do not reconcile and its energy balance does not close, and in [7] the wind energy is mistyped by a factor of 10 and some renewable fractions exceed 1.

Soloptima draws two conclusions from this. First, every simulation now checks its own results against plausible ranges, covering the performance ratio, capacity factors, fuel per kWh, emissions per litre, the energy balance, LCOE, payback, battery cycles and round trip, and lists anything outside them, so the same slips are caught in user projects (gap 22). Second, a model is only as good as its check against the field, and Soloptima's own validation rested on parity with its earlier engine and a comparison with PVGIS, neither of which is field data. Metered PV output can now be imported and compared with the model by hour and by month, with MBE, RMSE, R² and the monthly deviation, outage hours flagged, and soiling and availability fitted and applied to the project (gap 4).

A few reporting features came from the better-presented studies. Al Garni et al. [13] build a seasonal load from an appliance list, Omar [15] gives a monthly genset table and a histogram of battery state of charge by season, and [12] states the renewable fraction by capacity, energy and peak. All four are now in Soloptima (gap 25). For uncertainty studies the script console samples wind speeds from a Weibull distribution and other inputs from a lognormal one, after [10] (Eqs. 1, 2 and 21 to 26), with the Weibull sampling corrected as noted below.

## Corrections to the printed equations

Where a printed equation is wrong or incomplete, Soloptima uses the correct form.

- [10], Eq. 8 (reactive power flow) has the sine and cosine terms swapped. The correct form is V_i Σ V_j (G_ij sin θ_ij − B_ij cos θ_ij). The sweep load flow does not need this equation directly.
- [10], Eq. 19 contains "θ_i − θ_i", which is read as θ_i − θ_j.
- [10] samples Weibull wind speeds as v = C (ln r)^(1/k), which is invalid since ln r is negative. Soloptima uses v = C (−ln(1 − r))^(1/k).
- [10], Eq. 23 prints the first PV branch with H², where the standard form is P_sr · H / H_std. Soloptima uses its own PV model in any case.
- [10], Eq. 39 (quadratic approximation) uses squared differences in the numerator. Soloptima uses the three-point quadratic minimiser x* = ½ [(q2² − q3²) f1 + (q3² − q1²) f2 + (q1² − q2²) f3] / [(q2 − q3) f1 + (q3 − q1) f2 + (q1 − q2) f3], guarded against a near-zero denominator and clipped to the bounds.
- The spotted-hyena update in [10] leaves out the cluster average. Soloptima uses the form of [21], in which the new position is the mean over the best-N cluster of P_k − E·|B·P_k − X| and h falls linearly from 5 to 0.
- [10], Eq. 46 for the grasshopper step is the raw swarm model, which cannot optimise as it stands. Soloptima uses the form of [22], x_i = c·Σ c·(ub − lb)/2 · s(d_ij)·(x_j − x_i)/d_ij + T̂, with c falling from 1 to 10⁻⁵ and s(r) = 0.5 e^(−r/1.5) − e^(−r).
- [6], Eq. 11 should divide the discharge by η_d, and Fig. 4 prints "E_bmax + E_b" where a minus belongs.
- [6], Eq. 1 applies both P_r and η_PV, which counts the efficiency twice, and Eq. 41 (LOLP) divides energy by hours. Neither is used.
- [7], Eq. 12 (CRF) mixes rates. Soloptima uses one discount rate.
- [7], Eq. 19 needs each hourly deficit clamped at zero.
- In [29] the operating-mode test must leave room for the positive-sequence current, Vg− ≤ Z·(Imax − |I+|).
